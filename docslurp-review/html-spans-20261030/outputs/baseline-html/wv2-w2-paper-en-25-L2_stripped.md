# Thermal activation and operational stability of Thermomyces lanuginosus lipase

# immobilized on amine-functionalized SBA-15 silica

## Abstract

Lipase B–type hydrolases are workhorses of fine-chemical synthesis, yet their use above 50 °C is limited by rapid unfolding. We immobilized Thermomyces lanuginosus lipase (TLL) on SBA-15 silica grafted with 3-aminopropyl groups (pore diameter

- 8.6 nm) and compared the kinetics and thermal stability of the carrier-bound enzyme with the free enzyme between 30 and 70 °C. Immobilization raised the apparent Km toward p-nitrophenyl butyrate by 45 % at 30 °C, consistent with mild internal diffusion limitation, but lowered the apparent activation energy from 52.4 to 44.1 kJ mol⁻¹. First-order deactivation at 65 °C slowed 5.5-fold, extending the half-life from 30.0 to 165.0 min. After ten 4-h batches of tributyrin hydrolysis the immobilized preparation retained 86.9 % of its initial activity with less than 2 % protein leaching. These results support SBA-15-NH₂ as a carrier for continuous lipase processes operated at 55–65 °C.

Keywords: lipase; enzyme immobilization; mesoporous silica; thermal deactivation; Arrhenius kinetics

### 1. Introduction

Enzymes offer selectivity that is hard to match with chemical catalysts, and the steady improvement of protein engineering has widened their industrial reach [1]. Lipases (triacylglycerol hydrolases, EC 3.1.1.3) are among the most widely used enzymes because they accept a broad range of esters and remain active in organic solvents. The lipase from Thermomyces lanuginosus (TLL) is produced cheaply at scale and is used in detergents, oil modification and biodiesel production [3].

Free lipases are, however, difficult to recover and lose activity above roughly 50 °C. Immobilization on a solid carrier addresses both problems: the biocatalyst can be filtered off and reused, and multipoint attachment or confinement can rigidify the protein against unfolding [2, 9]. Ordered mesoporous silicas such as SBA-15 are attractive carriers because their pore diameter (5–10 nm) can be matched to the size of the enzyme and their surface is easily functionalized [4, 5].

Previous work from our group showed that amine grafting increases TLL loading on SBA-15 by electrostatic attraction at pH 7.5, where the carrier is positively charged and the enzyme (pI ≈

- 4.4) carries a net negative charge [6]. Whether the same preparation also improves operational stability at elevated temperature was not examined. Thermal behaviour is decisive for process design: a 10 °C increase in operating temperature roughly doubles the reaction rate and lowers the viscosity of oil substrates, but only pays off when the catalyst survives long enough to amortize its cost [12, 16].

Here we report a systematic comparison of free and immobilized TLL between 30 and 70 °C. We determine Michaelis–Menten constants at five temperatures, fit Arrhenius parameters, measure first-order deactivation at three temperatures and test reusability in repeated batches. Structural information from differential scanning fluorimetry, carried out at Hanbit National University, is used to interpret the stabilization mechanism [7].

- 2. Materials and methods

### 2.1 Chemicals and enzyme

TLL (Lipozyme® TL 100 L, 100 kLU g⁻¹) was a gift from a commercial supplier and was desalted on PD-10 columns before use. Pluronic P123, tetraethyl orthosilicate (TEOS, ≥ 99.0 %), (3- aminopropyl)triethoxysilane (APTES), p-nitrophenyl butyrate (pNPB) and tributyrin were obtained from standard suppliers and used as received. All buffers were prepared in ultrapure water (18.2 MΩ cm).

Figure 1. Glassware set-up for the hydrothermal synthesis and APTES grafting of SBA-15.

### 2.2 Carrier synthesis and functionalization

SBA-15 was synthesized following Zhao et al. [5]: 4.0 g P123 was dissolved in 150 mL of 1.6 M HCl at 35 °C, 8.5 g TEOS was added dropwise and the mixture was stirred for 24 h, aged at 100 °C for 48 h, filtered and calcined at 550 °C for 6 h (ramp 1 °C min⁻¹). For grafting, 2.0 g of dried silica was refluxed with 2.0 mL APTES in 60 mL dry toluene for 12 h under nitrogen, washed with toluene and ethanol and dried at 80 °C. Nitrogen physisorption gave a BET surface area of 612 m² g⁻¹ and a BJH pore diameter of 8.6 nm after grafting (742 m² g⁻¹ and 9.1 nm before).

Small-angle X-ray scattering showed the (100), (110) and (200) reflections of a well-ordered hexagonal mesostructure with a unitcell parameter of 11.4 nm. Thermogravimetric analysis under air indicated a mass loss of 7.9 % between 200 and 650 °C after grafting, corresponding to 1.32 mmol of aminopropyl groups per gram of silica. Elemental analysis (N 1.81 wt %) agreed within 3 % with this value.

### 2.3 Immobilization

Carrier (100 mg) was suspended in 10 mL of 50 mM sodium phosphate buffer, pH 7.5, containing 2.5 mg mL⁻¹ protein and shaken at 25 °C and 150 rpm for 4 h. Protein in the supernatant was quantified by the Bradford assay [11]; the loading was 198 ± 6 mg g⁻¹ carrier, corresponding to an immobilization yield of 79 %. The particles were washed three times with buffer and stored at 4 °C. Adsorption isotherms measured between 0.25 and 4.0 mg mL⁻¹ initial protein followed the Langmuir model with a maximum capacity of 236 mg g⁻¹ and an affinity constant of 3.1 mL mg⁻¹. The loading used in this study therefore corresponds to about 84 % of monolayer saturation, a compromise between volumetric activity and crowding of enzyme molecules at the pore entrances. ### 3.1 Kinetic constants

Table 1 summarizes the kinetic constants. At 30 °C immobilization increased the apparent Km from 0.42 to 0.61 mM, while kcat decreased by 13 %. An increase in Km is commonly observed for porous carriers and reflects the concentration gradient that develops inside the pores when the reaction is fast compared with diffusion [15]. The catalytic efficiency kcat/Km of the immobilized enzyme was therefore about 40 % lower than that of the free enzyme at all temperatures.

The difference in Km narrowed at higher temperature (0.68 vs. 0.75 mM at 70 °C) because substrate diffusivity increases and, for the free enzyme, partial unfolding during the 3-min assay begins to lower the apparent affinity. Above 60 °C the initial-rate traces of the free enzyme were visibly curved, so the 70 °C values for free TLL should be read as lower bounds.

### 3.2 Activation energy

Linear regression of ln kcat against 1/T (R² ≥ 0.995) gave Ea = 52.4 ± 1.3 kJ mol⁻¹ for the free enzyme and 44.1 ± 1.1 kJ mol⁻¹ for TLL@SBA-NH₂. The corresponding Q10 values between 40 and 50 °C were 1.83 and 1.66. A lower apparent activation energy after immobilization is a known signature of partial diffusion control, since the observed rate then scales with the square root of the intrinsic rate constant [15]. We nevertheless consider a contribution from a more open lid conformation likely, because the Thiele modulus estimated for 8.6-nm pores and 200 mg g⁻¹ loading is only 0.7 [8].

Plotting the efficiency ratio of bound to free enzyme against temperature yields a slope close to zero (−0.002 K⁻¹), so the relative penalty of immobilization does not depend on temperature in the studied range. This is useful for process design, because the kinetic data obtained at 30 °C can be scaled to the operating temperature with the Arrhenius parameters alone.Differential scanning fluorimetry showed a shift of the apparent

### 3.4 Protein retention

Leaching was assessed by incubating the biocatalyst for 24 h in buffers of increasing ionic strength. Below 0.2 M NaCl less than 1 % of the bound protein was released; at 0.5 M NaCl the release rose to

- 6.8 %, as expected for an electrostatically held enzyme. Under the low-salt conditions of the reuse experiments, retention is therefore not limiting, but feeds with high salt content would require a covalent post-treatment, for example with glutaraldehyde.

Figure 3 shows the residual activity of the immobilized biocatalyst over ten consecutive 4-h batches of tributyrin hydrolysis at 55 °C. Activity decreased almost linearly by about 1.4 percentage points per batch and reached 86.9 % in the tenth batch. Cumulative protein leaching was 1.7 % of the initial load, which accounts for only a small part of the decline; the remainder is attributed to slow thermal deactivation and to mechanical loss of fines during centrifugation (about 0.5 mg per cycle).

carriers: immobilization costs about 40 % of catalytic efficiency at a given temperature but buys a five-fold gain in lifetime. For a continuous process the relevant figure of merit is the total turnover number before the activity falls below an economic threshold, which scales with kcat/kd. On this basis TLL@SBA-NH₂ outperforms the free enzyme by a factor of 4.6 at 65 °C and 4.4 at 55 °C. The stabilization obtained here is moderate compared with covalent multipoint attachment on glyoxyl-agarose, which can raise half-lives by two orders of magnitude [14, 22]. Covalent methods, however, often cost 50 % or more of the initial activity and require long incubations at pH 10. The electrostatic route used here is fast, operates at neutral pH and keeps most of the activity. A rough cost estimate illustrates the practical relevance. With a carrier cost of about $40 per kilogram and a loading of 0.2 g protein per gram, the material cost of TLL@SBA-NH₂ is dominated by the enzyme itself. Each additional batch at 55 °C therefore lowers the catalyst cost per kilogram of product almost proportionally, as long as the activity loss per cycle stays near 1.4 percentage points. At this rate, 25 cycles would be possible before the activity falls below 65 %, a common replacement threshold in batch oleochemical processes.A second practical consequence concerns the choice of operating temperature. Because the apparent activation energy of the bound enzyme (44 kJ mol⁻¹) is much smaller than its deactivation energy (91 kJ mol⁻¹), raising the temperature shortens the lifetime faster than it increases the rate. For the immobilized preparation the optimum of the productivity per catalyst charge lies near 55 °C, about 10 °C higher than for the free enzyme.

Our results differ from those of Yamamoto and Ito [17], who reported substantial leaching of TLL from amine-grafted silica. Their material had a pore diameter of 5.4 nm, smaller than the enzyme's longest dimension (≈ 6 nm), so most protein was adsorbed on the external surface. With 8.6-nm pores, the majority of the enzyme resides inside the channels, where it is both protected and retained.

Limitations of the present study include the use of a single, activated model substrate and the batch format of the reuse tests. Packed-bed experiments with vegetable oil, where pore blocking by glycerol and long-chain products is expected, are under way in a 250-mL column reactor [16].

Finally, the comparison with literature values is complicated by the variety of activity units in use. We report turnover numbers per titrated active site rather than units per gram, so that the effect of the carrier on the intrinsic activity can be separated from the effect of loading. Expressed per gram of biocatalyst, TLL@SBA-NH₂ reached 1.9 kU g⁻¹ at 55 °C with tributyrin, within the range reported for commercial immobilized lipases [9, 18].

Taken together, the data show that a simple, one-step adsorption onto a well-chosen carrier captures a large part of the stability gain

usually associated with more elaborate immobilization chemistry.

### 5. Conclusions

Electrostatic immobilization of TLL in amine-functionalized SBA-15 with 8.6-nm pores yields a biocatalyst with a 5.5-fold longer halflife at 65 °C, a 5.6 °C higher apparent melting temperature and 86.9 % residual activity after ten batches. The modest loss of catalytic efficiency is largely explained by internal diffusion and is outweighed by the gain in stability. SBA-15-NH₂ is therefore a promising carrier for lipase processes operated between 55 and 65 °C.
