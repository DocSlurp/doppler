## Abstract

Riparian bu er strips are a mainstay of agri-environment schemes in England, but their e ectiveness in permeable chalk catchments, where much of the nitrate travels as deep groundwater, remains uncertain. We monitored four bu er strips of contrasting width and vegetation in the Wensum catchment, Norfolk, between October 2024 and September 2025 using 32 nested piezometers. Flow-weighted mean nitrate-N concentrations at the )eld edge ranged from 9.6 to 14.2 mg L⁻¹. Removal e0ciency increased with bu er width and woody vegetation, from 18–26 % in a 6 m grass strip to 64–78 % in a 20 m woodland strip, and was lowest in winter, when shallow 5ow paths were short-circuited by high water tables. A )rst-order transport model reproduced the observed concentrations with Nash–Sutcli e e0ciencies of 0.72–0.86. Our results suggest that, on permeable soils, strips narrower than 12 m deliver limited bene)t unless they are planted with deep-rooting woody species.

## 1. Introduction

Di2use nitrate pollution from arable land remains the main reason why many English rivers fail to reach good ecological status under the Water Framework Directive. In the Anglian river basin district, more than 70 % of the land area is designated as a Nitrate Vulnerable Zone (NVZ), and groundwater nitrate concentrations in parts of the chalk aquifer continue to rise decades after fertiliser inputs were reduced (Environment Agency, 2024). Vegetated bu2er strips along watercourses are promoted as a low-cost mitigation measure. Their nitrate-removal capacity rests on two processes: plant uptake in the root zone and microbial denitri cation in waterlogged, carbonrich soils near the stream (Hill, 1996; Burt et al., 1999). Both depend on groundwater actually passing through the biologically active layer. Where Low paths are deep, as is typical of permeable chalk and sandy soils, much of the

nitrate may bypass the bu2er altogether (Vidon and Hill, 2004).

Meta-analyses report a wide spread of removal eNciencies, from almost zero to over 90 %, with bu2er width explaining only part of the variation (Mayer et al., 2007). Subsurface hydrology, soil texture and vegetation type appear at least as important. Field data from lowland chalk catchments are scarce, and most British studies have been carried out on clay soils with predominantly shallow Low (Sabater et al., 2003; Dosskey et al., 2010).

This study addresses that gap. We asked (i) how nitrate removal in bu2er strips on a permeable chalk catchment varies with bu2er width and vegetation, (ii) how removal changes through the hydrological year, and (iii) whether a simple rst-order transport model can reproduce the observations well enough to support the design of new strips.

## 3. Results

- 3.1 Hydrology

Water tables responded quickly to winter rainfall. At WB1

and WB2 the water table rose to within 0.3 m of the surface in January and February 2025, while at WB4 it remained between 0.6 and 1.1 m below ground throughout the year. Hydraulic gradients towards the stream were steepest in winter (0.021–0.034) and reversed brieLy at WB3 in August, when the river was fed by upstream releases.

Mean lateral speci c discharge was highest at the sandy grass sites (0.08 m d⁻¹) and lowest at the woodland site (0.01 m d⁻¹). Residence times within the strips, estimated from v and bu2er width, thus ranged from about 23 days at WB1 to more than four years at WB4. This di2erence of two orders of magnitude is the key to the contrasting removal described below.

Dissolved oxygen in the shallow piezometers fell below 2 mg L⁻¹ at the stream edge of WB3 and WB4 for most of the year, whereas the grass strips remained oxic (> 5 mg L⁻¹) except during short waterlogging episodes in January. Redox potential followed the same pattern.

## 4. Discussion

Our results con rm that bu2er width matters, but they also show that width alone is a poor predictor of removal on permeable soils. The 12 m willow strip removed considerably more nitrate than the 12 m grass strip on similar soils, and the gain was largest in summer and autumn, when willow roots were active below 1 m. This agrees with Polish and Danish experience with short-

rotation coppice on arable margins (Wiśniewski and Kowalczyk, 2021).

The strong seasonality has practical consequences. Most nitrate leaves arable elds in late autumn and winter, exactly when removal in the strips is at its lowest. In annual-load terms, winter accounted for 46 % of the nitrate entering WB2 but only 31 % of the nitrate removed. Policies that credit bu2er strips with a xed removal percentage are therefore likely to overestimate their bene t in chalk catchments.

Residence time emerged as the unifying control. When the four sites are pooled, removal eNciency increases with the logarithm of residence time (r² = 0.81, n = 16). Long residence times result either from low conductivity, as at WB4, or from greater width. This suggests a simple design rule: on sandy loams with K above 2 m d⁻¹, strips should be at least 15–20 m wide or planted with woody vegetation to achieve 50 % removal in winter.

Several limitations should be noted. Our monitoring covered a single, climatically average year, and the deep Low paths below 2.5 m were not sampled. Some nitrate may have passed beneath all four strips unseen; groundwater dating at a nearby borehole indicates that water at 10–15 m depth is 15–25 years old (Papadopoulou and Whit eld, 2023), so legacy nitrate will continue to reach the river irrespective of bu2er management. Finally, N₂O emissions, which could o2set part of the waterquality bene t, were not measured.

Despite these caveats, the contrast between sites was consistent across seasons and was reproduced by a parsimonious model, which gives us con dence in the main conclusions.## 5. Conclusions

- Removal eNciency increased from about 20 % in a 6 m grass strip to over 70 % in a 20 m woodland strip.

- Woody vegetation roughly doubled removal compared with grass at the same width.

- Removal was lowest in winter, when most nitrate leaves arable land.

- A rst-order transport model calibrated per site and season reproduced the data with NSE ≥ 0.72.

- On permeable chalk soils, strips narrower than 12 m deliver limited bene t unless they are planted with deep-rooting species.
